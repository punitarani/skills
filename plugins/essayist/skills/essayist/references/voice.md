# Voice: rhythm, moves, and the anti-slop pass

Structure gets a piece read; voice gets it quoted. This file covers the sentence-level
habits observed across the exemplar corpus, then the AI-tell patterns to scrub. Apply
while drafting — retrofitting voice onto flat prose produces flat prose with tics.

## Rhythm targets

- **Average sentence 11–17 words, with violent variance.** Roughly 20–25% of sentences
  under 8 words; a few 30–70-word sentences per piece. The music is whiplash: a long
  cumulative sentence piling concrete clauses, snapped shut by a verdict. *"…and
  counting every share issued as a transfer from owners to employees."* → *"AI should
  change the shape of the company."*
- **Punch sentences land where weight lands.** Aphorisms and verdicts close the
  paragraphs that carry the argument — *"The logo is the whole point."* *"Worlds take
  time."* — but if nearly every paragraph ends on a short verdict, the rhythm becomes a
  metronome, which is its own tell. Vary it: some paragraphs trail off into the next
  thought; some open on the verdict.
- **Paragraphs: 1–4 sentences in argument mode, 4–8 in narrative mode.** Single-sentence
  paragraphs are reserved for the thesis, laws, and pivots — a few per piece.
- **Long sentences are cumulative, not nested.** They add concrete clauses with commas
  and colons, never subordinate mazes. Colons do definitional work: *"Translating a spec
  into code, testing, debugging: the rules are complex but they are rules."*

## Signature moves

- **The two-sentence chiasm.** The genre's atomic unit: second sentence flips one word
  of the first. *"A copilot sells the tool. An autopilot sells the work."* *"Existing
  products will not break. They will fall behind."* Use it for the coined frame or the
  close; more than ~3 per piece dulls the blade — count them at audit. And never build
  yours on an excerpt's template: if your aphorism matches "X sells the A. Y sells the
  B." word-shape-for-word-shape, recast it.
- **Concede-then-reverse.** Steelman in ≤3 sentences, then the "But" carries the claim.
  *"It's not that there's no logic to this… But for a lot of AI companies that instinct
  is exactly backwards."* Never enumerate pros and cons; give the objection one
  respectful beat and a decisive turn.
- **One governing metaphor, made load-bearing.** Lighthouse ships and land; toys and
  boxes; doors, rails, towns. It names sections, names traps, and returns at the close.
  Other metaphors appear once, work, and retire ("prune the leaves," "no-man's land").
  AI writing decorates with many metaphors; this genre operates one.
- **Petty specificity.** Not "small businesses" but "the controller in Des Moines."
  Not "affordable" but "$12.99 against a $500 service call." Not "recently" but "when I
  turned 50 last fall." Details slightly too specific to invent are the signature of
  witnessed prose — which is why they must come from research, never imagination.
- **Unhedged stance, personally owned.** Claims in present tense with definite articles:
  *"Inference is the COGS of intelligence."* When hedging, hedge once, personally, then
  commit: *"But I think the more interesting thing about AI is…"* followed by a flat
  claim. Banned: "arguably," "it's worth noting," "some might say."
- **Register breaks.** Strategy prose interrupted by colloquial spikes: "death march,"
  "watch your VPs like a hawk," "angrily arguing with a chatbot," "Good luck!" One or
  two per piece. Parentheticals carry personality: "(or pay customers to use them!)",
  "(The industry norm is between six and nine months.)"
- **Enumeration in prose.** "First… Second… Third…" or "One… Two… Three…" inside
  sentences, not bullet lists. *"If a task is already outsourced, it tells you three
  things. One, the company has accepted…"*
- **Rhetorical questions rationed.** 1–3 per piece, each immediately answered, each
  doing structural work as a pivot ("So what's different now?"). Never decorative.
- **Named humans.** Colleagues cited by name ("As Chris Dixon said…"), founders by first
  name ("Tarek, Luana, and the Kalshi team"), conversations recounted ("Eugenia Kuyda
  said something to me that I keep coming back to"). Only when real — see research.md.
- **Insider vocabulary unglossed.** SBC, ARR, CSAT, RCM — fluency is assumed for the
  audience. Only genuinely novel terms get defined, and the definition itself carries
  voice ("candidly because Task Economy has such a nice ring to it").

## Formatting discipline

- **No bulleted lists in argument.** Across ~6,500 words of the a16z corpus there is not
  one argumentative bullet list. Lists are licensed only for: a framework's parts, a
  flow/pipeline, a sector map with bold lead-ins, tactical checklists in operator memos.
  Everything else is prose — even seven failure modes run as bold-led paragraphs.
- **Bold** for coined terms on first use, headers, and the structural lead-ins the mode
  licenses (trap names, map verticals, diagnostic questions). In operator memos,
  mid-sentence bolding of the operative clause is allowed. Never bold for general
  emphasis scatter.
- **Italics** for term definitions and single-word stress (*one* job, *don't*). Pick
  bold or italics as the piece's marker; don't alternate randomly.
- **Em dashes ≤2 per 1,000 words.** The corpus authors vary (one uses 18 per piece, one
  nearly zero), but em-dash density is now the most recognized AI tell, so default low:
  prefer a period, comma, colon, or parentheses; keep an em dash only where the pivot
  genuinely needs it. Zero is acceptable, not required — one em dash doing real work
  reads more human than sterile absence. No " -- " doubles.
- **Straight quotes,** consistent header case, no emojis.

## Calibration excerpts

Before drafting, reread a few of these. They are the register:

- "The next $1T company will be a software company masquerading as a services firm."
- "That logo you're chasing is costing you your market."
- "People are happier when they make things."
- "When we talk about AI today, tokens are king."
- "A paintbrush has never saved anyone a single minute, and we've kept them within reach
  for tens of thousands of years."
- "The wedge is abandoned work: no budget line to justify, no incumbent to displace,
  just found money."
- "A poll is a still photo taken on the afternoon someone happened to ask. An index is
  a continuous recording."
- "You can rent a TPU, but you can't buy one, or rack it in your own data center, or
  build a business on it."
- "50 unhappy customers means churn. 500 is a reputation problem."
- "People lie on surveys. They ignore ads. They abandon carts. But when they spend,
  save, send, borrow, or repay, that's the truth."
- "A thousand dollars per engineer per month is not excessive; it is close to table
  stakes."
- "It is a harsh model. It is not a cultural blueprint for every founder. But it is a
  reminder that radical cost discipline, product simplification, and price realization
  are possible. The strong form exists."
- "Grow 10 or earn 40. Build the next product wave or build the cash machine. No middle
  lane. Good luck!"
- "The toys were fine. But we always wanted to play with the boxes."

What these share: assertion, concrete nouns, earned shortness. What none of them do:
hedge, announce themselves, or gesture at "the landscape."

**Calibration only.** These are other writers' published prose: never reuse or closely
paraphrase a line in a draft, and never borrow a line's sentence template — they
calibrate register, not syntax.

## The anti-slop pass

Run `scripts/slop_check.py` for the mechanical layer (it carries the full banned-pattern
list with word-boundary matching), then scan for the judgment patterns below. Any real
hit gets rewritten, not decorated. Two standing exemptions: a flagged word inside
quoted material or a proper noun is evidence or a name, not slop (review, don't
rewrite) — and a term of art used precisely ("leverage ratio," a literal landscape)
stands.

**Banned vocabulary** (the tic list; rewrite the sentence, don't synonym-swap): delve,
tapestry, testament, underscores, highlights (as verb of significance), pivotal,
crucial, vital, landscape/ecosystem (abstract), vibrant, robust, seamless, leverage
(verb), foster, garner, showcase, elevate, empower, unlock (figurative), game-changer,
paradigm shift, "in today's fast-paced/rapidly evolving world."

**Banned constructions:**
- *-ing analysis tails:* "…, highlighting the importance of…", "…, underscoring…",
  "…, showcasing how…". The clause pretends to analyze; cut it or make the claim
  directly.
- *Vacuous negative parallelism:* "It's not just X, it's Y" where nobody claimed X.
  Negation is licensed when the negated half is a real belief the essay argues against —
  the concede-then-reverse, or *"Existing products will not break. They will fall
  behind."* As a free intensifier, it's slop.
- *Rule-of-three padding:* triads of abstract nouns ("innovation, inspiration, and
  insights"). Concrete triads with real referents are fine ("Fan fiction. A kid DJing
  in his bedroom. Some ridiculous game you and your daughter invent on a rainy
  Saturday.") — the test is whether each item is checkable.
- *Vague attribution:* "Experts argue," "observers note," "many believe." Name the
  person or cut the claim.
- *False ranges:* "from X to Y" where X and Y aren't on a scale.
- *Copula avoidance:* "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," "features." Write "is" and
  "has."
- *Signposting:* "Let's dive in," "Here's the thing," "The real question is," "At its
  core." Do the thing instead of announcing it.
- *Spec leakage:* this skill's working vocabulary may not appear in the piece —
  "concession," "narrows the claim," "callback," "steelman," "enemy belief," "coined
  frame." Make the concession without labeling it a concession, and enter it a
  different way each time: a named skeptic, the strongest dataset against you, a failed
  case, a flat "Here is what this argument gets wrong."
- *Aphorism formulas:* "X is the Y of Z," "the currency of," "the architecture of" —
  unless the piece has earned it with a concrete mechanism (as "Inference is the COGS
  of intelligence" does: the essay explains the accounting). The formula without the
  mechanism is fortune-cookie.
- *Staccato drama:* more than two consecutive sub-5-word sentences. One hammer blow,
  not a drum roll.
- *Summary conclusions:* any closing paragraph that restates the sections, or ends on
  "exciting times ahead," "the future is bright," "only time will tell."
- *Chatbot residue:* "I hope this helps," "Let me know," "In this article we will."

**Fabrication check (highest severity):** the piece may not contain any fact, number,
name, date, or quote that isn't in the evidence bank (traced to a specific verified
line) or from the user. This includes facts added during revision — enrichment gets the
same bank check before it ships. A vague true sentence beats a specific invented one,
every time. If a passage needs a fact you don't have, that's a research task, not a
writing task.

**The final read:** read the whole piece aloud (mentally). Mark any sentence you
stumbled on, any paragraph that could open any essay on any topic, any place depth is
uniform where a human would compress the dull part and dwell on the live one. Fix
those. Uniformity of attention is itself a tell.
