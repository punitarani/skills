# Structure: the skeleton, the modes, and the parts

Derived from close reading of 11 exemplar essays (Sequoia: "Services: The New Software,"
"America's Open-Model Paradox," "From Hierarchy to Intelligence," "Listen to the Market";
a16z: "Lighthouse or Landgrab," "How to Win the Largest Market in AI," "The World-Building
Doors Are Open, Again," "The Most Human Technology Ever Made," "There Are Only Two Paths
Left for Software," "Unbundling the BPO"; Everett Randle's "The Task Economy" X article).
The patterns generalize to the whole genre — Stratechery, Not Boring, firm blogs, the
best long-form X. Treat them as defaults with reasons, not laws — and see SKILL.md's
"Genre flex" note before applying them to non-market pieces.

## The shared skeleton

Every exemplar runs some version of this spine. Dwell time per stage varies wildly;
order barely does.

1. **HOOK (0–60 words).** A compressed claim, image, or coined pattern — never
   scene-setting for its own sake. The first sentence could be the tweet. A specific
   proper noun or number lands in or immediately after the hook (inside the first ~150
   words). Four hook types:
   - One-sentence contrarian claim as its own paragraph: *"The next $1T company will be
     a software company masquerading as a services firm."*
   - Second-person accusation: *"That logo you're chasing is costing you your market."*
   - Named historical law: *"There's a certain rhythm in the history of computing: we
     can call it the specialization turn."*
   - Concrete scene/personal arc that earns a coined phrase by paragraph's end
     (the Roman army opening; "I've always been fascinated by solving problems").

   Before committing, draft two candidate hooks of *different types* and keep the
   better. Do not default to the dated-anecdote scene every time; if the strongest
   artifact in the evidence bank is a stat, open on the stat.
2. **DRAMATIZE THE STATUS QUO (1–3 paragraphs).** Show the default belief in concrete
   motion — founders courting Fortune 100s, everyone measuring tokens, "consumer was
   pretty dead." The essay argues against a belief the reader already holds, never into
   a vacuum.
3. **CONCEDE, THEN REVERSE.** The signature pivot. Steelman the default in 1–3 sentences
   — *"It's not that there's no logic to this…"*, *"That technology is wonderful…"* —
   then the "But" carries the thesis: *"…exactly backwards."* Never skip the concession;
   the reversal has no force without it.
4. **NAME THE FRAME (by word 250–400).** The coined frame, typographically marked on
   first use (bold or italics), compressed into a quotable line. When it's a dichotomy,
   the two-sentence parallel aphorism works: *"A copilot sells the tool. An autopilot
   sells the work."* When it's a category, law, or metric, compress differently — a
   definition with voice, a one-line law.
5. **EVIDENCE BLOCKS.** 2–4 sections of named cases and numbers (micro-structure below).
   This is where the research bank gets spent.
6. **MECHANISM / FRAMEWORK APPLICATION.** How it works operationally — the 2x2, the
   numbered playbook, the sales-motion mechanics, the "First… Second… Third…" build.
   One enumerated framework per piece (3 or 4 parts), in prose or one restrained list.
7. **OBJECTION BEAT.** One of three flavors (see below), handled briefly and decisively.
   Plus exactly one **concession paragraph** somewhere that narrows and strengthens:
   *"Distillation does not explain China's entire open-model lead. But…"*
8. **TURN IT ON THE READER.** A direct question or if/else fork: *"What does your company
   understand that is genuinely hard to understand?"* / *"If they need proof, go win the
   logo… If they need math, get on a plane."*
9. **CLOSE = CALLBACK + FORWARD MOTION (≤60 words).** The planted opening image or phrase
   returns transformed, then a short kinetic forward-looking line, then (persona
   permitting) a CTA. *"Tokens are king"* → *"tasks will be king."* Never a recap.
   Callback + forward motion does **not** mean "restate the coined frame as a final
   chiasm" — rotate the close across image / dare / fork / CTA, and if the fork already
   appeared at stage 8, don't close on a second fork.

## The four modes

Pick one at Step 2. Modes share stages 1–4 and 9 exactly; they differ in the middle.

### Mode A — Framework / strategy essay
1,800–2,500 words. 4–8 headers. For: operator guidance, market playbooks, "how to pick."
Distinctives: mirrored sections for each side of the coined binary (definition ¶ → two
named cases → operational-mechanics ¶); a decision section with 2 bold diagnostic
questions combining into a named 2x2 (name all four quadrants, including the two that
matter less); a **traps section** — 3–4 failure modes per side as bold-led prose
paragraphs with metaphor-consistent names ("Pilot purgatory," "Grabbing land you can't
hold"); a sequencing section resolving the binary over time; concrete thresholds
throughout ("If they run longer than 60 days…").

### Mode B — Idea / vision essay
1,000–1,600 words. No headers — continuous prose jointed by one-line pivot questions
("So what's different now?") or flat contrarian corrections ("But the feed was slop
before any machine got involved."). For: reframes, cultural arguments, "what this is
really about." Distinctives: a universal human claim ≤8 words up top, grounded in two
domestic/concrete images; a remembered conversation or borrowed thinker delivering the
reframe; a chain of 4–6 short movements each pivoting on a reversal; one intellectual
authority + one hyper-specific citizen example + one personal anecdote; a "Here's what
I think happens when…" synthesis paragraph before the close. Evidence here is fewer,
sharper entities — data-light is correct for this mode; generic is not.

### Mode C — Announcement earned by a thesis
900–1,600 words. 0–2 headers. For: investment/product/joining announcements, launches.
Distinctive: the announcement is **withheld** until 40–60% through; the first half is a
standalone market argument that would survive without the announcement. The reveal
arrives as the argument's inevitable conclusion, not its premise. Mechanics get one
dense paragraph followed by a deflation move ("The mechanics matter less than what they
produce."). Gratitude names real people by first name. CTA links the thing announced.

### Mode D — Native X / Substack article
1,500–2,500 words. Headers allowed (X articles support them). For: personal-brand
essays, category-naming pieces. Distinctives: the **first ~50 words double as the
preview card** — the hook must complete inside them (the fully-armed thesis can wait
until ~word 250); the title carries the entire share decision (prediction + number or
coined term: "The Task Economy — Data Will Be the Next $1 Trillion Category"); more
"I," candid meta-asides ("candidly because Task Economy has such a nice ring to it");
no deal-flow CTA — the piece itself is the instrument; if data came from someone,
credit graciously ("the team at Mercor was kind enough to provide…"). One artifact
engineered to be the screenshot *if you can actually produce one the venue renders* —
a real chart built from the evidence bank when charting tools are available, or a clean
text table without links. If not, skip it and leave a bracketed chart spec for the user;
the compressed aphorism is the screenshot. Never invent data for an artifact.

### Compressing a mode

When the requested length sits below a mode's floor, keep the spine and the mode's
signature beat (Mode A: the two bold diagnostic questions; Mode C: the withheld reveal;
Mode D: the preview-card hook) and drop distinctives in this order: sequencing section
first, then the traps section, then the named 2x2, then the mirrored per-side sections.
Say in the notes file which distinctives were cut for length.

## Titles and deks

Write the title last; offer 2–3 options. The title is the argument, not the topic.
Standalone questions and product names live in the dek, not the title (a question may
precede a promise: "Lighthouse or Landgrab? How to Pick Your AI Sales Strategy").
Never "A Guide to," never hedged. Observed formulas:

- **X: The New Y** — "Services: The New Software"
- **Coined binary + how-to promise** — "Lighthouse or Landgrab? How to Pick…"
- **Superlative / finality claim** — "The Most Human Technology Ever Made," "There Are
  Only Two Paths Left for Software"
- **From X to Y** — "From Hierarchy to Intelligence"
- **Imperative** — "Listen to the Market"
- **Named paradox/economy + forecast** — "America's Open-Model Paradox," "The Task
  Economy — Data Will Be the Next $1 Trillion Category"
- **Framework verb + colon + promise** — "Unbundling the BPO: How AI Will Disrupt
  Outsourced Work"

Dek: 1–3 sentences. Rotate its shape — a single payoff sentence, a question ("To
Distill, or Not to Distill?"), or a facts-then-verdict stack — rather than defaulting
to the same stack every piece.

## Openers

The seed of the thesis lands inside the first 60 words; the fully-armed thesis by word
250–400 (for Mode D the *hook* must complete inside the first ~50 words; thesis by
~250). A concrete number or named entity appears within the first 150 words, always.
The only licensed delay is a ramp doing evidentiary work (the 1,300-word Roman-army
history) — and even that opens with a hook sentence that could stand alone.

## Headers

Optional below ~1,600 words; expected above ~2,000 when a framework needs navigation.
When used: 2–8 of them per the mode, bold inline style, either compressed coined terms
("The Lighthouse," "Copilots and Autopilots") or complete argumentative sentences ("The
Weights Are Open. Our Dependency Is Not."). One pun header per piece is allowed ("The
Adjusted Jig Is Up"). Capitalization can be title case or sentence case — pick one and
stay consistent. Never "Introduction," "Background," "Conclusion," never gerund titles
("Exploring the…"), never numbered headers. In headerless pieces, one-line pivot
questions do the header's job.

## Evidence-block micro-structure

Each named case runs: **company + what it does + the skeptic's prior + linked metric +
one-line moral.** ("Twitter was a dumb site where people posted what they had for
breakfast… now one of the most important information networks in the world. Worlds take
time.") Every hard number arrives with an inline link and a translation in the same
sentence ("roughly 300× what it had been processing two years earlier"). Charts, when
present, sit after the claim they prove and are never narrated.

For sector maps (the "Opportunity Map" pattern): bold lead-in per vertical, TAM in
brackets with honest ranges "($50–80B outsourced in the US alone)", one structural
dynamic, 1–3 named startups — including obscure seed-stage ones; obscurity is the
credibility.

## Objection-beat flavors

Run exactly one, briefly:

- **Proof of existence** — a case study proving the hard path is walkable ("It Can Be
  Done: The Lesson of Broadcom… The strong form exists.")
- **Incumbents will respond, with a deadline** — grant the incumbents their evidence,
  then bound the window ("This opportunity has an expiration date.")
- **Why has nobody noticed** — explain the market's blind spot in two reasons (spend
  hidden inside labs; no public proxy metric). Makes a thesis feel like a scoop.

## Closes

≤60 words, two beats: callback + forward motion. The strongest observed closes compress
the thesis into a chant or fresh aphorism first: *"Grow 10 or earn 40. Build the next
product wave or build the cash machine. No middle lane. Good luck!"* / *"But your buyer
doesn't purchase the future; they purchase either proof or math."* CTA appears only when
the persona earns it (see below). No summary, no "exciting times ahead," no throat-clear.

## Persona defaults

Persona changes pronouns, credible evidence, and the CTA — SKILL.md Step 0 carries the
one-line versions; these are the full defaults:

- **Investor / firm voice.** "We" for the firm's beliefs, "you" to founders. Credible
  evidence: portfolio access, market maps, proprietary charts, colleague citations by
  name. CTA: direct sourcing ask with real contact ("If your main competition is a BPO,
  we'd love to chat." / "reach out. julien@…"). Can publish a founder's guest essay with
  a short firm preface.
- **Founder / operator.** "I" for judgment and war stories, "you" to peer operators.
  Credible evidence: own metrics, customer stories, what you've seen ship. CTA: the
  product, hiring, or nothing. Never fake market-map omniscience — the authority is
  lived specifics.
- **Independent analyst / writer.** "I" sparingly for judgment ("I think the more
  interesting thing…"), universal "we/you" otherwise. Credible evidence: sourced data,
  linked reporting, named conversations. CTA: none or subscribe; the piece is the brand.
